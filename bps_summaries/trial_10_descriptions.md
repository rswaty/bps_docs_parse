# Trial: 10 short BpS descriptions

Drafts for the Conservation Gateway pilot. Each paragraph uses one model and follows `short_bps_description_parameters.md`. Wording is limited to the geographic range, biophysical site, vegetation, disturbance, and species tables in that model. Return intervals, map zones, acreages, and soil series are left out.

## Central Florida Pine Flatwoods

Model `14530_55_56`.

Found in peninsular Florida, from about Levy and St. Johns counties south to Hillsborough, Osceola, and Polk counties, on nearly level flatwoods along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts and between the central ridges. The pine canopy ranges from open and savanna-like to nearly closed, over a low shrub layer and a grassy ground cover, with older trees in patches and gaps. Frequent surface fire is the main disturbance and tops the shrub and ground layers. Hurricanes can thin or blow down stands, and flooding matters where these flatwoods meet wetlands. Characteristic plants are longleaf pine, slash pine, saw palmetto, wiregrass, and gallberry.

## Atlantic Coastal Plain Peatland Pocosin and Canebrake

Model `14520_58_60`.

Found on the outer coastal plain from southeastern Virginia through North Carolina into South Carolina, mostly in North Carolina, on domed peatlands, peat-filled Carolina bays, and other peat-filled depressions. Deep peat carries a low evergreen shrub thicket with sparse, stunted pond pine. Shallower peat carries taller shrubs and pond pine woodland, and cane was part of that woodland where fire was frequent. Fire is the main disturbance. It typically kills the aboveground shrubs and trees, which then resprout, and in drought it can burn into the peat and leave herbaceous openings. Characteristic plants are pond pine, swamp titi, gallberry, fetterbush, and switchcane.

## Acadian-Appalachian Alpine Tundra

Model `13860_66`.

Found on the higher summits of the northern Appalachian Mountains, from northern New England and the Adirondacks into the Gaspé, with scattered occurrences south into southern New England, above treeline. Most of the cover is dwarf-shrubland, lichen, or sparse vegetation, with taller shrubs in protected spots and small bogs in depressions. Wind, snow, and cold are the main disturbances and act year-round. Landslides and snowslides are rare, and these summits rarely have enough fuel to burn. Characteristic plants are bog blueberry, pincushion plant, and Bigelow's sedge.

## Laurentian Pine-Oak Barrens

Model `14071_41_50_51`.

Found in Michigan and Wisconsin on sandy glacial outwash and sandy glacial lakeplains along the climatic tension zone. The barrens are a savanna mosaic, from brush and scrubby oak and pine in grassland to park-like woods of widely spaced oaks and pines, grading into prairie on one side and dry forest on the other. Frequent fire is the main disturbance and keeps the canopy open. Frost and drought also limit woody growth in some places. Characteristic plants are white oak, black oak, northern pin oak, jack pine, and eastern white pine.

## East-Central Texas Plains Post Oak Savanna and Woodland

Model `15190_32_35_36_37`.

Found in east-central Texas, between the Blackland Prairies and the East Texas pineywoods, on a gently rolling woodland plain. Historically the landscape was a mosaic of tallgrass savanna, open woodland, and denser oak forest, with woody thickets more common toward the south. Frequent surface fire is the main disturbance. It burns through the grass and is tied to drought. Characteristic plants are post oak, blackjack oak, little bluestem, big bluestem, and indiangrass.

## Chihuahuan Creosotebush Desert Scrub

Model `10740_25_26`.

Found in the Chihuahuan Desert of southern New Mexico, west Texas, and northern Mexico, on flat to gently sloping basins, alluvial plains, and lower piedmont slopes. In the north it has spread into former desert grassland. The vegetation is a sparse to moderate shrub layer, usually dominated by creosote bush, with little grass. Drought is more important than fire in this system. Fires are infrequent and generally of low severity. Characteristic plants are creosote bush, American tarwort, lechuguilla, and black grama.

## Columbia Plateau Scabland Shrubland

Model `10650_1_8_9_10`.

Found mainly in the Channeled Scablands of the Columbia Plateau in Washington, between the Spokane, Columbia, and Snake rivers, with small occurrences in southeastern Oregon, far western Idaho, and extreme northern Nevada. It occupies very shallow soils and exposed fractured basalt, and bare rock usually covers most of the ground. The vegetation is an open dwarf-shrubland of scabland sagebrush and Sandberg bluegrass, and plant cover is low. Fire rarely carries because fuels are discontinuous. Drought and frost heaving are the main stresses. Characteristic plants are scabland sagebrush, Sandberg bluegrass, buckwheat, and bitterroot.

## Northern Rocky Mountain Ponderosa Pine Woodland and Savanna

Model `10530_10_19`.

Found in the northern and central Rocky Mountains of Montana, central Idaho, and northeastern Washington, on hot, dry, south- and west-facing lower slopes. Frequent fire maintained a ponderosa pine overstory, a grass understory, and sparse shrubs. Frequent surface fire is the main disturbance. Mixed-severity fire is more common on moister, steeper slopes, and western pine beetle can attack large pines. Characteristic plants are ponderosa pine, antelope bitterbrush, Idaho fescue, and bluebunch wheatgrass.

## California Coastal Redwood Forest

Model `10150_2_3_4`.

Found in a narrow coastal band from the Chetco River in Oregon south to Monterey County, California, on the west side of the northern California Coast Range. The largest trees grow in moist, wind-protected canyons and on lower slopes. In the drier south the forest is limited to coves and ravines. These are dense forests dominated by coast redwood. An inland form includes Douglas-fir and tanoak, and a wetter fog-belt form includes Sitka spruce and western hemlock. Surface fire is the main disturbance and usually stays in the understory. Windstorms, landslides, and flooding also kill trees. Characteristic plants are coast redwood, Douglas-fir, tanoak, Sitka spruce, and western hemlock.

## Hawaii Lowland Dry Forest

Model `18130_79`.

Found on the leeward, rain-shadow slopes of the main Hawaiian Islands. The forest ranges from open to dense and includes many trees, with shrubs, ferns, and vines. Younger stands may be dominated by 'ohi'a lehua. Older stands are dominated by lama, less often by kauila, and wiliwili may dominate the driest sites. Windthrow, drought, fire, and landslides open the canopy, and repeated fire can convert the forest to shrubland or grassland. The model also lists the Polynesian rat and fuelwood cutting as disturbances that open the forest. Characteristic plants are 'ohi'a lehua, lama, kauila, and wiliwili.

## Notes from this trial

These ten names each have a single model, so nothing here tests merging several map-zone writeups into one paragraph.

Common names come from the model species tables when those tables name the plant. Two exceptions: pincushion plant is *Diapensia lapponica* and Bigelow's sedge is *Carex bigelowii*. The alpine model names those species and gives a common name only for bog blueberry. Gallberry is the name used in the flatwoods and pocosin narratives; the species tables call the same plant inkberry. American tarwort, scabland sagebrush, and koaoha are the common names stored on those models.

The Laurentian species table also lists northern red oak, pin oak, and red pine. The vegetation narrative names white oak, black oak, northern pin oak, jack pine, red pine, and eastern white pine as the canopy trees. Red pine was left off the five-name list. Northern red oak and pin oak are not described as canopy dominants in that narrative.
